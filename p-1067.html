Challenger 300 Overview

When Bombardier set out to build a clean-sheet business jet at the turn of the millennium, the result reshaped an entire category. Announced in 1999 as the Continental and renamed the Challenger 300 in 2002, the aircraft entered service in 2004 and quickly came to define the super-midsize class – offering a cabin nearly 39 percent larger than the midsize jets it competed against, at a price and operating cost that made it one of the best-selling business jets of its generation.

The Challenger 300 is a super-midsize business jet powered by two Honeywell HTF7000 turbofans, each producing roughly 6,826 pounds of thrust. That power gives it genuine coast-to-coast capability: about 3,065 nautical miles of range with a full cabin, enough to connect New York and Los Angeles nonstop, at a maximum cruise speed of Mach 0.82. It climbs directly to a 45,000-foot ceiling and, in 2004, set a class speed record on a transcontinental run – performance that still holds up against newer designs.

What makes it a strong aircraft to own is the same thing that makes it worth managing well. The cabin – with a flat floor, stand-up height, and seating for eight or nine passengers – was class-leading at its introduction and remains a benchmark for space and comfort, with an in-flight-accessible baggage compartment. Its balanced field length of roughly 4,800 feet at sea level gives it access to airports that larger jets cannot use, while its Rockwell Collins Pro Line 21 avionics and proven Honeywell engines have earned it a reputation for reliability. In Bombardier’s lineup it sits directly above the Learjet 60 and below the heavy Challenger 604 – and with 457 built and the great majority still flying, it remains one of the most active and in-demand aircraft in its class.
