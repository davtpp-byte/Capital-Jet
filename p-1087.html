Gulfstream G200 and G280 Overview

The Gulfstream super-midsize story begins with the Galaxy, an aircraft designed by Israel Aerospace Industries and introduced in 1999. When Gulfstream acquired the program in 2001, it became the G200 – and it gave Gulfstream, long known for its large-cabin flagships, a genuine foothold in the super-midsize class. Around 250 were built through 2011. Powered by two Pratt & Whitney Canada PW306A engines, the G200 cruises at up to Mach 0.85 with a range of approximately 3,400 nautical miles, and its defining trait is space: a wide, stand-up cabin that seats eight to ten passengers and feels a class above its operating costs.

The G280 is its successor, and while the two share a lineage, they are very different aircraft. Launched in 2008 and certified by the FAA in 2012, the G280 was substantially redesigned by Gulfstream – a new wing based on the G550 planform, a T-tail, more powerful Honeywell HTF7250G engines, and the PlaneView280 flight deck built on Collins Pro Line Fusion. The result improved on the G200 across nearly every measure. The G280 delivers 3,600 nautical miles of range, enough to fly the U.S. East Coast to Western Europe nonstop, at a maximum cruise speed of Mach 0.85, and it climbs quickly to a 45,000-foot cruise altitude.

What makes the G280 especially capable is its runway and approach performance. With a balanced field length of roughly 4,750 feet, the highest thrust-to-weight ratio of any Gulfstream, and certification for steep approaches, it operates comfortably from short runways and high-altitude airports that constrain other super-midsize jets. Its cabin, stretched over the G200’s and lit by nineteen windows, is among the quietest in its class. In both cases – the G200’s exceptional cabin volume and the G280’s range and versatility – the qualities that make these aircraft worth owning are the same ones that make disciplined, knowledgeable management worthwhile.
